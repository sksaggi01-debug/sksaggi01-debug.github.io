Part 1. The benchmark item

SubtopicAuction theory / mechanism design
LevelGraduate (first-year PhD micro)
DifficultyHard
Expected solve time12–15 minutes

Stem

An indivisible asset worth nothing to its owner is offered to three risk-neutral bidders whose private values are independently and uniformly distributed on [0, 1]. The owner runs a sealed-bid first-price auction and announces, before bidding, the reserve price that maximizes her expected revenue. Bidders play the symmetric Bayesian Nash equilibrium. What is the owner's expected revenue?

Answer choices

  • AThe optimal reserve is zero with three bidders, so expected revenue equals the expected second-highest value, 1/2.
  • BThe optimal reserve is 1/2 and expected revenue is 5/12 ≈ 0.417.
  • CThe optimal reserve is (n − 1)/n = 2/3 and expected revenue is 1/2.
  • DThe optimal reserve is 1/n = 1/3 and expected revenue is 14/27 ≈ 0.519.
  • EThe optimal reserve is 1/2 and expected revenue is 0.598, the equilibrium bid of a bidder with value 0.8.
  • FThe optimal reserve is 1/2 whatever the number of bidders, and expected revenue is 17/32 ≈ 0.531.
  • GThe optimal reserve is 1/2 and expected revenue is 17/28 ≈ 0.607.
  • HAny reserve is irrelevant in a first-price auction, so expected revenue equals the expected highest value, 3/4.
  • IThe optimal reserve is 1/2 and expected revenue is 15/32 ≈ 0.469.
  • JThe optimal reserve is 2/5 and expected revenue is 657/1250 ≈ 0.526.

Rationale

By revenue equivalence the first-price auction with reserve r yields the same expected revenue as a second-price auction with the same reserve, so revenue is the expected virtual value of the winner: R(r) = n∫r1(2v − 1)vn−1dv, since the virtual value for U[0,1] is v − (1 − v)/1 = 2v − 1; the optimal reserve sets the virtual value to the seller's value of zero, r* = 1/2, independent of n (Myerson 1981); with n = 3, R(1/2) = 3[(1/2 − 1/3) − (1/32 − 1/24)] = 3(1/6 + 1/96) = 17/32 ≈ 0.531. A omits the reserve (1/2); B is the two-bidder value; C and D use reserves that scale with n, and C's reserve 2/3 happens to return exactly the no-reserve revenue; E reports one bidder's bid b(0.8) = 0.8 − (0.8³ − 0.5³)/(3 × 0.8²) ≈ 0.598; G conditions on a sale, dividing by 1 − (1/2)³ = 7/8; H confuses revenue with the expected highest value; I is 1 − 17/32; J evaluates R at r = 0.4.

Verified: closed-form values with exact fractions for every option; a 400,000-draw simulation of the second-price format with reserve 1/2 returned 0.5314.

Citations

  • Myerson, R. B. (1981). Optimal auction design. Mathematics of Operations Research, 6(1), 58–73.
  • Riley, J. G., & Samuelson, W. F. (1981). Optimal auctions. American Economic Review, 71(3), 381–392.
  • Krishna, V. (2010). Auction Theory (2nd ed.). Academic Press. Chapters 2 and 5.

Part 2. Peer review of a submitted item

Item under review (constructed for this sample)

Two firms sell a homogeneous good. Market demand is Q = 100 − P and both firms have constant marginal cost 10. Firm 1 can produce at most 30 units; Firm 2 has no capacity limit. Firms set prices simultaneously. What is the equilibrium price?

Submitter's key: P = 10, "the Bertrand paradox holds because Firm 2 can serve the whole market."

Review: Bertrand competition with a capacity constraint

1. Topic relevancy. Pass. Price competition with capacity constraints is graduate industrial organization.

2. Self-containment. Fail. The stem does not state a rationing rule. When Firm 1 is the low-price firm and cannot meet demand, the residual demand facing Firm 2 depends on which consumers Firm 1 serves. The answer changes with the rule, so the item cannot be solved as written.

3. Originality. Pass. Not a standard textbook exercise in this form.

4. Independent solution. The key is wrong under efficient rationing, the standard assumption. At P₁ = P₂ = 10 both firms earn zero. If Firm 2 raises its price to p, Firm 1 sells its 30 units and Firm 2 faces residual demand 70 − p, earning (p − 10)(70 − p), maximized at p = 40 with profit 900 > 0. So (10, 10) is not an equilibrium. Nor is any pair of prices above 10: whichever firm is higher is undercut, and at Firm 2's residual-monopoly price of 40, Firm 2 gains by undercutting Firm 1 (profit ≈ 1,797 vs 900). There is no pure-strategy equilibrium; the equilibrium is in mixed strategies (Edgeworth cycle logic, Kreps and Scheinkman 1983; Levitan and Shubik 1972).

5. Answer-choice distinctness. Not assessable until the ask is fixed; the current key would make the correct statement ("no pure-strategy equilibrium exists") appear as a distractor.

Decision: Needs revision.

Revision instructions. (1) State efficient rationing in the stem. (2) Replace the ask with one that has a single verifiable answer, for example the upper bound of the mixed-strategy price support (40, Firm 2's residual-monopoly price) or Firm 2's equilibrium expected profit (900). (3) Rewrite the rationale to show why (10, 10) fails, and add a distractor at P = 10 for solvers who apply the unconstrained Bertrand result.

Verification checklist

  • Solved independently before reading the key: yes
  • Deviation from (10, 10) checked numerically: (40 − 10)(70 − 40) = 900
  • Undercutting check at p = 40: (100 − 39.9)(39.9 − 10) ≈ 1,797 > 900
  • Missing assumption identified: rationing rule
  • Citations checked: Kreps & Scheinkman (1983), Bell Journal of Economics 14(2), 326–337; Levitan & Shubik (1972), International Economic Review 13(1), 111–122
