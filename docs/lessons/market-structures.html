What Are Market Structures?
FOUNDATION CONCEPTS
Why It Matters

Market structure describes how a market is organised — how many sellers exist, how much control they have over price, and how easy it is to enter. Understanding this helps us explain why wheat farmers earn little profit while pharmaceutical companies earn billions.

🟦 Perfect Competition

Many sellers, identical products. No single firm can influence the market price. Each firm is a price taker.

Key conditions:

① Many buyers & sellers
② Identical (homogeneous) products
③ Free entry & exit
④ Perfect information
⑤ No transport costs

Price = MC Normal profit LR Allocatively efficient
🟥 Monopoly

One seller dominates the market. The firm is a price maker — it sets price by choosing output level.

Key conditions:

① Single producer
② Unique product (no close substitutes)
③ High barriers to entry
④ Price maker
⑤ Supernormal profit possible in LR

P > MC Supernormal profit Deadweight loss
Key Formulas at a Glance
Perfect Competition: P = MR = AR = MC (at profit max)
Monopoly: MR < AR = P (at profit max: MR = MC, but P > MC)
Profit = (P − ATC) × Q
Real-World Case Studies
APPLYING THEORY TO THE REAL WORLD
🌾 Case Study 1 — The Wheat Market (Perfect Competition)

Background: Kansas wheat farmers sell into one of the world's closest approximations of a perfectly competitive market. In 2023, roughly 25,000 wheat farms operated in Kansas alone, each producing virtually identical Grade 1 Hard Red Winter Wheat.

Key facts:

• Market price is set globally on the Chicago Mercantile Exchange — no single farmer influences it.
• If farmer A charges $0.01 more per bushel than the market price, buyers switch instantly.
• Entry & exit: Any landowner can plant wheat next season with minimal capital.
• In 2022–23, unusually high demand (Ukraine war) temporarily pushed profits above normal. New farmers entered and acreage expanded, pushing price back toward average cost by 2024.

Economic outcome: In the long run, economic (supernormal) profit = $0. Farmers earn only a normal profit — enough to cover opportunity cost. Resources are allocated efficiently: P = MC.

Discussion question: Why do wheat farmers keep farming if economic profit is zero in the long run?

💊 Case Study 2 — EpiPen Pricing (Monopoly Power)

Background: Mylan Pharmaceuticals held near-exclusive rights to sell the EpiPen auto-injector in the United States. From 2007 to 2016, the price of a two-pack rose from approximately $100 to over $600 — a 500% increase.

Why was Mylan a price maker?

• Patent protection created a legal barrier to entry.
• No close substitutes existed in an easy-to-use auto-injector format.
• Demand was inelastic — patients with severe allergies had to buy.
• Brand loyalty and prescribing habits locked in consumers.

Economic outcome: Mylan earned supernormal profits for years. Price was set far above MC, creating a significant deadweight loss — many low-income families could not afford EpiPens. Congress held hearings in 2016; generic competitors eventually entered, and price fell.

Discussion question: Was patent protection socially beneficial or harmful in this case? Can you think of arguments on both sides?

💻 Case Study 3 — Microsoft Windows (Natural Monopoly & Network Effects)

Background: By the late 1990s, Microsoft Windows held over 90% of the desktop operating system market. The U.S. Department of Justice sued Microsoft in 1998 for anti-competitive behaviour.

How did the monopoly form?

• Network effects: The more people used Windows, the more valuable it became (software developers wrote for Windows because users used it; users chose Windows because software existed for it).
• Switching costs: Once businesses trained staff on Windows, switching to a rival OS was expensive.
• Bundling Internet Explorer with Windows squeezed rivals like Netscape out of the browser market.

Economic outcome: Microsoft set prices above competitive levels and maintained supernormal profits. The 2001 settlement required Microsoft to share its APIs with competitors. Today, mobile operating systems (iOS, Android) have disrupted desktop dominance.

Discussion question: Are network-effect monopolies different from patent monopolies? Should they be regulated the same way?

🐟 Case Study 4 — Fish Auction Markets (Near-Perfect Competition)

Background: Fish auctions in Japan (e.g., Tsukiji/Toyosu) and the Netherlands (Urk fish market) are textbook examples of near-perfect competition. Hundreds of fishing boats sell similar grades of fish to hundreds of buyers each morning.

Conditions met:

• Large number of buyers and sellers — no single party controls price.
• Fish of the same species and grade are virtually homogeneous.
• Auction format ensures transparent, real-time price discovery.
• Prices fluctuate daily based on the previous night's catch and buyer demand.

Economic outcome: Prices quickly reflect supply and demand. A large catch drives prices down; a storm that reduces supply drives prices up. Profit margins are thin, and in bad years, some fishing boats exit the market.

Discussion question: What would happen to this market if one large company bought all the fishing boats? Draw a diagram to illustrate the change.

Diagram Guide
UNDERSTANDING THE GRAPHS

🟦 Perfect Competition — Short Run

Firm is a price taker. If P > ATC, supernormal profit exists — attracting new entrants.

🟥 Monopoly — Profit Maximising

Monopolist sets MR = MC, then reads price off demand curve. P > MC creates deadweight loss (DWL).

How to Read These Diagrams

Step 1 — Find the profit-maximising output: Where MR = MC (vertical dashed line).

Step 2 — Find the price: In perfect competition, P is given (horizontal line). In monopoly, read up from Q* to the demand curve.

Step 3 — Identify profit/loss: If P > ATC → supernormal profit (shaded rectangle). If P < ATC → loss.

Step 4 — Check for deadweight loss: In monopoly, output is restricted below the competitive level. The triangle between the competitive Q and monopoly Q, between MC and demand, is the deadweight loss to society.

⚡ The Dynamic Efficiency Debate

Monopolies are statically inefficient (P > MC, DWL) but can be dynamically efficient. Supernormal profits fund research and innovation — think pharmaceutical breakthroughs or tech giants' AI investment. This is why patents grant temporary monopoly power as an innovation incentive.

The policy challenge: how long should a patent last? Long enough to reward innovation, but not so long that consumers are overcharged indefinitely.
