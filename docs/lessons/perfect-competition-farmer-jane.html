Section 1

Meet Farmer Jane

Every morning before sunrise, Jane Kowalski steps out of her farmhouse in western Kansas, surveys her 640 acres of wheat, and checks her phone for one number: the price posted on the Chicago Board of Trade (CBOT). She does not set this price. She does not negotiate it. She simply accepts it — and plans her entire day around it.

Jane has been farming wheat for eleven years. She is skilled, hardworking, and deeply knowledgeable about her land. Yet despite all of this, she has almost no control over how much money she makes per bushel. That number is decided thousands of miles away, by the collective force of millions of buyers and sellers operating simultaneously across global commodity markets.

Jane is, in economic terms, a price taker — one of the clearest examples of this concept that exists in the real world. Her farm is our lens for understanding one of the most powerful and elegant ideas in economics: perfect competition.

"In a perfectly competitive market, the individual seller is like a single grain of sand on a vast beach. Visible up close, but invisible to the tide."

Section 2

Why Wheat is a Textbook Perfect Market

Economists define perfect competition as a market structure with four specific characteristics. Wheat farming satisfies all four remarkably well.

Condition 1

Many Buyers and Many Sellers

Thousands of independent farmers operate across Kansas, Oklahoma, Nebraska, and Texas. Hundreds of flour mills and food processors compete to purchase the harvest. No single farmer can shift the total supply; no single buyer can move the market price.

Condition 2

Homogeneous Product

Grade No. 2 Hard Red Winter Wheat is a standardised commodity. A bushel from Jane's farm is chemically and functionally identical to one from 300 miles away. Flour mills test for protein content and grade — not for who grew it. There is no "premium wheat brand."

This is fundamentally different from Apple selling iPhones. Apple charges a premium because its product is perceived as different. Jane cannot.

Condition 3

Free Entry and Exit

There are no government licences, patents, or proprietary technologies that prevent new farmers from entering the wheat market. If prices rise, neighbours convert fields to wheat. If prices fall and losses persist, farmers switch to corn or soybeans.

Condition 4

Perfect Information

The CBOT publishes wheat futures prices in real time, accessible to anyone. Jane sees the same price data as the largest grain trading company in the world. No secret deals, no hidden price sheets — every participant decides with the same public data.

Section 3

The Revenue Side — Simple but Powerful

Because the market sets Jane's price, her revenue structure is straightforward. If the CBOT posts wheat at $6.50 per bushel, every bushel she sells — her first, her ten-thousandth, her thirty-thousandth — earns exactly $6.50. Not more for selling more. Not less for selling less. Always $6.50.

This makes Jane's demand curve perfectly horizontal — a flat line at the market price. Her marginal revenue — the extra revenue earned from selling one more bushel — always equals the market price. Every additional bushel adds exactly $6.50 to her total revenue.

Section 4

The Cost Side — Where the Real Decisions Live

Fixed Costs are the expenses Jane pays regardless of output — her land mortgage, combine harvester, irrigation infrastructure, insurance, and property taxes. These total approximately $40,000 per year whether she grows 1,000 or 100,000 bushels.

Variable Costs change directly with output — seeds, fertiliser, pesticides, fuel, and seasonal labour. These rise as Jane grows more wheat, but they do not rise in a straight line.

The first 20,000 bushels are relatively efficient to produce. The next 10,000 require more effort — pushing into less fertile land, running machinery harder, hiring overtime labour. Each additional bushel becomes progressively more expensive. This is the law of diminishing returns at work.

Section 5

Profit — Finding Jane's Sweet Spot

Profit is simply Total Revenue minus Total Cost. At a market price of $6.50/bu:

Output (Bushels)Total RevenueTotal CostProfit / LossVerdict
10,000$65,000$68,000–$3,000Loss
20,000$130,000$108,000+$22,000Profitable
30,000$195,000$160,000+$35,000⭐ Max Profit
40,000$260,000$240,000+$20,000Profitable but falling
50,000$325,000$350,000–$25,000Loss

Jane's profit peaks at 30,000 bushels — the point where the gap between total revenue and total cost is widest. Revenue alone does not determine profit; it is the relationship between revenue and cost that matters.

Section 6

How the Market Corrects Itself

Perfect competition is self-correcting. No regulator or committee is needed — supply, demand, and the free movement of farmers in and out of the industry do the work automatically.

Stage 1 — High Prices, High Profits
An export boom pushes wheat to $8.00/bu. All wheat farmers earn healthy profits. Word spreads. New farmers begin converting fields to wheat.
Stage 2 — New Entry Increases Supply
More farmers enter → total supply grows → buyers have more options → price begins to fall from $8.00.
Stage 3 — Falling Prices Squeeze Margins
As prices fall below $6.50, farmers with higher cost structures begin operating at a loss and consider exiting.
Stage 4 — Exit Reduces Supply
Higher-cost farmers exit → supply shrinks → prices stabilise and begin to recover. The cycle continues.
Stage 5 — Long-Run Equilibrium
The market settles where efficient farmers earn a normal return — covering all costs but no extraordinary windfall. Price settles near the minimum cost per bushel: approximately $5.33.

💡 The Long-Run Paradox

In the long run, perfectly competitive firms earn zero economic profit. This does not mean farmers earn nothing — it means they earn exactly what their time, land, and capital would earn in their next best alternative. There is no excess, no windfall.

The consumer is the true beneficiary. Prices stay as low as possible, production is as efficient as possible, and resources flow to their most productive use.

Section 7

What This Means for Jane's Strategy

Jane cannot raise her price. She cannot advertise her way to a premium. She cannot patent her wheat variety. The only lever she has is her cost structure. Everything else is outside her control.

  • Jane's only competitive lever is producing at a lower cost per bushel than her rivals.
  • Any cost advantage is temporary — perfect information means competitors will copy it.
  • The market price is set externally; her revenue per bushel is always the market rate.
  • Profit is maximised where the gap between total revenue and total cost is greatest.
  • In the long run, entry and exit push the price toward the minimum cost per bushel.
