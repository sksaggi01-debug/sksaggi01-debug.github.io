Activity 1 · Warm-Up Poll

What Does It Mean to Be a Price Taker?

Before we dive into numbers, let's check your intuition. Read the scenario and choose what you think Farmer Jane should do.

📋 Scenario: It's harvest season. The Chicago Board of Trade posts wheat at $6.50 per bushel. Farmer Jane is annoyed — her neighbor got $6.80 last month. She thinks her wheat is higher quality and wants to charge $7.00 per bushel to mills and food processors.

What will happen if Farmer Jane tries to charge $7.00?

🌾 Key Insight: Because wheat is a homogeneous product, buyers have no reason to pay more than the market price. This is exactly what "price taker" means — Farmer Jane faces a perfectly horizontal demand curve at $6.50. She has zero pricing power.

💬 Discussion Prompt

A classmate says: "If everyone in perfect competition eventually earns zero economic profit, what's the point of being efficient? Why bother cutting costs if the market just takes your gains away?"

How would you respond? Choose the best argument:

🎓 Key Takeaways to Remember:

1. Price takers, not price makers — In perfect competition, the market price is fixed externally. Your only lever is costs.

2. Profit = Revenue − Cost — Revenue grows at a constant rate (price × output). Costs rise faster as output expands. The sweet spot is where the gap is widest.

3. Profits attract entry; losses cause exit — The market corrects itself automatically. No regulator needed.

4. Long-run equilibrium = zero economic profit — Not zero accounting profit. You earn a fair return, but no windfall.
